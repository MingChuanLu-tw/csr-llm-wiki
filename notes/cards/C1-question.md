# Card C1 — The question and why it is open

Source page: [[Kopel2021-CSR]] §1 · [[first-mover-advantage]]

| # | Fact | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| C1.1 | A **CSR leader** commits to socially responsible investment before its competitor. | Kopel p. 490 |
| C1.2 | **FMA** is defined here as higher equilibrium profit: $\pi_1^* > \pi_2^*$. | Kopel pp. 490, 499–500 |
| C1.3 | Four research questions: does the leader always win; who has the higher incentive and the higher level; how do spillovers change it; how does competition change it. | Kopel p. 490 |
| C1.4 | The strategy literature on first/second-mover advantage is large, but very few CSR papers address it. | Kopel p. 490 |
| C1.5 | Optimistic side: an FMA is sustainable if the initiative is "central to the firm mission, visible to stakeholders and with firm-specific benefits beyond those of public goods". | Tetrault Sirsly & Lamertz (2008), quoted Kopel p. 490 |
| C1.6 | Skeptical side: CSR is hard to keep specific to a firm's strategy and to profit from in the long run. Vogel (2005) is *The Market for Virtue*. | Reinhardt (1998), Vogel (2005), per Kopel p. 490; title per `wiki/sources.md` |
| C1.7 | Industry practice, quoted **in full** (verified against p. 490, 2026-11-17): "First-mover advantage soon passes. After a while, for example, everybody turns green, and the winners are the companies with the best execution. One large consultancy advises its big clients to be number two or three on corporate responsibility rather than number one." | The Economist (2008), quoted Kopel p. 490 |
| C1.9 | Bibliographic: *Decisions in Economics and Finance* 44: 489–505; open access, CC BY 4.0. | Kopel p. 489 |
| C1.8 | ⚠️ The **conclusion** groups Tetrault Sirsly & Lamertz **with** the skeptics — the paper formalises their condition, it does not refute them. Full clause: "…difficult to sustain a first-mover advantage **based on investments in CSR**." | Kopel pp. 491, 502 |

**Do not** present TS&L as the losing side (A3 F1).
