# Appropriability / specificity of CSR

**Sources so far**: [[Kopel2021-CSR]] (1 of 5 planned)

## What it means

Appropriability is a firm's ability to **capture** the benefit of its own CSR
investment rather than let it become an industry-wide or public good. In
Kopel (2021) it is the economic content of the parameter $\theta$: high
appropriability ⇔ low [[csr-spillovers]].

The canonical definition, quoted by Kopel in footnote 6 (p. 494) from
Burke & Logsdon (1996, p. 497):

> Specificity refers to the firm's ability to capture or internalize the benefits
> of a CSR programme, rather than simply creating collective goods which can be
> shared by others in the industry, community or society at large

## Why it is the pivot of the whole paper

Kopel's result (Lemma 2, p. 500) is that a [[first-mover-advantage]] in CSR
exists **only** where appropriability is near-total.

**[our reading]** Appropriability is therefore not one factor among several — it
is the binary that decides whether CSR leadership pays at all, which reframes the
managerial question from *"should we invest in CSR?"* to *"can we hold on to the
benefit if we do?"* Kopel's own phrasing of the implication is that management
"has to aim for a good match between particular constellations of industry/firm
properties and investments in CSR" (p. 502).

## Worked examples from the paper

Footnote 7 (p. 494) gives two contrasting cases:

| Case | Outcome | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Patagonia** | differentiation sustained | sportswear and outdoor clothing; "quite successful in differentiating its products and defending its advantage in a premium-segment of the market" (fn. 7, p. 494) |
| **StarKist** (dolphin-safe tuna) | advantage lost almost immediately | the procurement policy was easy to copy; "its main competitors imitated this policy almost immediately" |

Footnote 6 adds an example of *high* specificity: "think of a firm which — instead
of air conditioning — uses water from a nearby lake to keep its offices cool and
comfortable. This not only serves the environment, but also the firm which can
strategically communicate these activities to stakeholders" (p. 494).

**[our reading]** Note that Kopel's stated benefit is strategic communication.
The reason the lake is *defensible* — a competitor without the same lake cannot
copy it — is our gloss, and it is the pattern we take from all three examples:
**appropriability comes from an asset the rival does not have**, a brand
position, a location, a process, rather than from the CSR act itself.

## What raises or lowers it

Only the first row is the paper's; the rest is **[our reading]**, extrapolated
from the examples above.

| Raises appropriability (lowers $\theta$) | Lowers it (raises $\theta$) |
|---|---|
| legal protection of the CSR activity (p. 494) | easily observed, easily copied practices |
| CSR tied to a firm-specific asset | industry-wide certification or standards |
| CSR central to brand identity | reputation spillovers across the industry |
| — | **convergent CSR** (see below) |

The last row is the uncomfortable one, and here the paper is explicit about
direction: the leader is the actor. "Industry leaders oftentimes pressure and
support less socially responsible competitors to imitate their CSR practices"
(p. 491), out of "fear of negative reputation spillovers" (p. 502). Firms may
rationally **give up** their own appropriability to defend the industry's shared
reputation — which, Kopel notes, "makes it impossible for the CSR leader to
profit from its leadership" (p. 502).

## Measurement problem

The model treats $\theta$ as a known scalar. In practice nobody observes it. The
paper concedes (p. 491) that it "might be difficult to show empirically which firm
has a higher incentive to engage in CSR, since the answer depends on factors which
are quite hard to measure accurately."

**[our reading]** Given that the first-mover advantage requires $\theta$ below
roughly $0.0015$ at $\gamma = 0.3$ (own calculation, `checks/a4_report.md` §3 —
not a number in the paper), and that $\theta$ is unmeasurable, the practical
content of the result is close to: *assume you will not get a first-mover
advantage unless you can point to the specific asset that protects it.* See
[[open-questions]] Q3.
